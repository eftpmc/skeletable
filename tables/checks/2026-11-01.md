### What the check caught

- **Utah was out of date, twice.** The first page we read was marked "Superseded 5/3/2023", and the blind reader said so. The next was marked "Effective until 5/1/2024". The table now uses the version effective May 1, 2024. The older text had no attorney general notice, so two cells would have been wrongly blank. A blank that hides a real duty is the worst kind of mistake this table can make, and our quote check can't see it, because the old page still has its quotes.
- **New Hampshire's low-harm exception.** The blind reader said no, because notice is also required when no determination can be made. We kept yes: a business that does determine misuse is unlikely needs no notice, which fits the column's rule. The quote now shows both halves. This cell stays read once.
- **West Virginia's encrypted data.** The blind reader said no, because a stolen key brings encrypted data back in. We kept yes, as the column's rule says stolen keys can undo this. This cell stays read once.

### What it couldn't reach

- **Alaska, Georgia, Michigan and Mississippi are blank throughout.** Alaska's and Michigan's official sites refuse us or ask bots to stay out, Mississippi's fails a certificate check, and we found no readable official text for Georgia. Michigan's law text does exist, in a search summary, and summaries don't count.
- **Older official text.** These can be behind the current law, so they're the next to recheck: Hawaii (2017 chapter), Tennessee (2017 act), New Mexico (2017 act), New Jersey (2005 act), Indiana (2024 code), Colorado (2024 statutes), Texas (2019 bill for its resident deadline) and New York (2024 bill). Utah's lesson applies to them.
- **Attorney general pages stand in for the law** in Arkansas, Connecticut, Illinois, Oklahoma and Washington, and those pages leave a lot unsaid. Oklahoma's says Senate Bill 626 requires telling its attorney general, and we couldn't read the bill. Illinois's quotes the law's timing but only gives day counts for state agencies and health care entities, so its business cells stay blank.
- **Justia refuses our reader**, so we didn't use it, even for states with nothing else.

### What the whole table shows

- 22 rows state a day limit: six say 30, eleven say 45 and five say 60. No state with a stated number we could read goes beyond 60.
- Thresholds for telling the attorney general run from 50 residents in DC to 1,000 in seven states.
- Blank doesn't mean no. Wisconsin, Kansas and Ohio have no attorney general cell because those pages don't mention such a duty, and our blind reader confirmed that for each. The table still leaves them blank rather than saying no, because a page's silence isn't a statement of law.

Two reader fixes came out of this batch. Numbers written as "one thousand" now count as stating 1000, and a PDF labelled `application/PDF` is now read as a PDF, which Kentucky's needed.

## What the sources couldn't give us

- **California's code site and New York's Senate site** refuse our reader, and **Washington** and **Colorado's attorney general** ask bots not to read them. We don't get around either.
- **California's two day counts** are second-hand. They come from an Assembly committee's June 2025 analysis of SB 446, not the law, and that analysis describes the bill as amended on May 14, 2025. A search summary says the signed law matches, but we haven't read the signed law.
- **New York's values** come from the 2024 bill, S2659-B, so they show what the bill said. A 2025 bill, A913, refers to it as a chapter of the laws of 2024. A913 changes who else must be told, and we don't know if it passed.
- **Texas's deadline to residents** comes from the enrolled 2019 bill, because the code page reads as an empty shell. The attorney general's page gives the current 30-day deadline for its own notice, which replaced the 60 days in that bill, so the 60 days for residents could have changed too.
- **Colorado's text** is the 2024 edition of its statutes, the newest we could find. Its page header calls it an uncertified printout.
- **Connecticut and Illinois's code sites** fail a security certificate check, so Connecticut comes from its attorney general's page. That leaves encryption and harm blank there, and they may well be in the law.

## What reading the sources showed

- **New York's attorney general still describes the old law.** Its SHIELD Act page says only "the most expedient time possible", with no thirty days, and a Department of State guide says the same. Anyone relying on those pages would miss the deadline.
- **Connecticut and Louisiana are both sixty days.** An early search summary gave 45 for both. We couldn't find that claim on any page we opened, so it isn't recorded as a conflict.
- **The attorney general clocks start in different places.** Louisiana's 10 days run from telling residents and Colorado's 30 from determining a breach happened. A column of numbers hides that, so each column's rule says to read the quote.

## How far to trust this

- A blank never means no. Massachusetts really does state no day count, and the blind reader agreed, but Texas's blank on encryption only means we couldn't read the code.
- We asked what these pages say, not how a court reads them. "Low-harm exception" in particular covers rules that work differently from state to state.
- The blind reader is also an AI, and no person has checked this check.

The rule for numbers changed for this table: a number can now be written in words in its quote, because laws say "thirty days". Words inside a longer number don't count, so "thirty-five" doesn't prove 30.
